Abstract

Institutional protocol and diplomatic symbolism constitute an insufficiently researched yet significant subject within security studies, diplomacy and state protocol. This paper offers a systematic comparative analysis of the institutional and symbolic continuity of Macedonian diplomatic protocol across three constitutive historical eras: the Yugoslav period (1944-1991), the period of independence and the struggle for international recognition (1991-2000), and the postmodern diplomatic period (2000-2026). Diplomats are considered as carriers and mediators of these institutional and symbolic practices rather than as the primary object of personnel-based analysis. The methodology is threefold: historical-descriptive analysis of the development of institutional protocol, comparative analysis through an analytical matrix of nine parameters, and application of the innovative “Protocol Thread” to trace four parallel protocol symbols (credentials, the flag, the formula of representation, and the reception ceremony) across the three eras. The primary source base consists of the monograph Diplomacy, Protocol and Security, 19 issues of Diplomatic Chronicle, and the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (1963). The central thesis is that protocol is not merely a formal instrument but an institutional DNA that sustains the continuity of Macedonian diplomatic representation across political transformations. The paper identifies three constitutive tensions — identity versus functionality, tradition versus innovation, and sovereignty versus integration — and argues that the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (1963) constitutes the most stable normative constant across all three eras.
